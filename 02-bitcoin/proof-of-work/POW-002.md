## 3. Executive Summary

Hashcash was designed to impose a small computational cost on the use of otherwise inexpensive Internet resources.

Its original target was systematic abuse such as:

- bulk email spam,
- abuse of anonymous remailers,
- denial-of-service pressure on server resources.

The core mechanism is simple:

1. The requester constructs a stamp containing context such as a date and resource identifier.
2. The requester varies a counter.
3. Each candidate stamp is passed through a cryptographic hash function.
4. Work continues until the resulting hash satisfies a difficulty condition.
5. The receiver verifies the stamp with one hash computation and several policy checks.

The central property is cost asymmetry:

```text
Generator

Many hash attempts

↓

Valid stamp

↓

Verifier

One hash attempt
```

For an honest user sending a small number of messages, the added work may be tolerable.

For an abusive sender attempting millions of requests, the aggregate cost becomes significant.

Bitcoin inherited the idea of searching for an input whose hash satisfies a configurable difficulty condition. Bitcoin then embedded that mechanism into a much larger consensus system involving blocks, transaction validation, cumulative chain work, economic incentives, and difficulty adjustment.

Hashcash is therefore an important ancestor of Bitcoin Proof of Work, but it is not itself a decentralized consensus protocol.

## 5. The Economic Problem Behind Hashcash

### 5.1 Unmetered Resources

An unmetered resource is one whose requester pays little or no marginal cost while the receiver or service operator bears a nontrivial cost.

Email illustrates this asymmetry.

A sender may incur almost no cost to transmit one additional message.

The recipients and infrastructure operators collectively bear costs such as:

- bandwidth,
- storage,
- filtering,
- malware inspection,
- user attention,
- abuse handling.

For ordinary users, the imbalance may be small.

For a sender transmitting millions of messages, it becomes economically important.

### 5.2 Hashcash's Design Response

Hashcash does not attempt to identify whether a sender is honest.

It changes the cost structure.

Instead of asking:

> Is this sender trustworthy?

Hashcash asks:

> Has this sender performed the required computation for this request?

The system converts access to a resource into a computationally priced action.

## 6. Computational Cost Functions

Hashcash belongs to a broader class of mechanisms known as computational cost functions or client puzzles.

A useful cost function should provide at least the following properties.

### 6.1 Configurable Cost

The verifier should be able to require more or less work.

In Hashcash, this is represented by the number of required zero bits.

### 6.2 Generation–Verification Asymmetry

Generating a valid proof should require many attempts.

Verification should require approximately one hash operation plus validation of the stamp fields.

### 6.3 Probabilistic Search

There should be no known shortcut substantially better than trying candidate values.

### 6.4 Context Binding

The proof should be tied to the intended resource.

Otherwise, work created for one recipient or service could be reused against another.

### 6.5 Limited Lifetime

A date or time field allows receivers to reject stale stamps.

### 6.6 Replay Detection

A verifier may need to record previously accepted stamps to reject repeated use.

## 7. Hashcash Version 1 Stamp

The official Hashcash documentation describes the version 1 stamp using the following structure:

```text
ver:bits:date:resource:ext:rand:counter
```

A representative form is:

```text
1:20:YYMMDD:resource::random-value:counter-value
```

The exact values vary according to the application and generated work.

---

## 8. Stamp Field Analysis

### 8.1 `ver`

```text
ver
```

Identifies the stamp format version.

For the version discussed here:

```text
ver = 1
```

Versioning allows the format to evolve without making older stamps impossible to identify.

---

### 8.2 `bits`

```text
bits
```

Specifies the required work level.

Conceptually, a valid stamp must produce a hash satisfying a condition equivalent to a required number of leading zero bits.

For example:

```text
bits = 20
```

means that approximately one out of every `2^20` uniformly distributed hash results is expected to satisfy the condition.

The expected number of trials is therefore:

```text
E[trials] ≈ 2^bits
```

Examples:

| Required bits | Expected trials |
|---:|---:|
| 16 | 65,536 |
| 20 | 1,048,576 |
| 24 | 16,777,216 |
| 32 | 4,294,967,296 |

Each additional required bit approximately doubles the expected work.

---

### 8.3 `date`

```text
date
```

Records the stamp's creation date or time using a defined timestamp format.

A verifier can use this field to enforce an acceptable age window.

The date serves several purposes:

- reject stale stamps,
- limit the useful replay period,
- support cleanup of replay-detection records,
- bind the work to a period of time.

The date is not sufficient by itself to prevent replay.

A receiver may still need to record already-used stamps.

---

### 8.4 `resource`

```text
resource
```

Identifies the resource for which the work was generated.

For email, this may be a recipient address.

For a network service, it may be a service identifier or another application-specific value.

Resource binding is critical.

Without it, an attacker could generate one valid proof and reuse it across multiple recipients or services.

Conceptually:

```text
Stamp for Recipient A
```

should not also be valid as:

```text
Stamp for Recipient B
```

---

### 8.5 `ext`

```text
ext
```

Provides space for optional extensions.

The version 1 documentation treats it as an extensibility field rather than part of the basic computational puzzle.

It may be empty:

```text
::
```

---

### 8.6 `rand`

```text
rand
```

Contains random data.

Randomization helps distinguish stamps that otherwise share the same version, difficulty, date, and resource.

It also allows multiple independent search spaces.

---

### 8.7 `counter`

```text
counter
```

Is varied by the generator while searching for a valid hash.

Conceptually:

```text
counter = 0
hash(stamp)

counter = 1
hash(stamp)

counter = 2
hash(stamp)

...

until valid
```

The counter is the search variable most directly analogous to the nonce-search concept commonly used to explain Bitcoin mining.

---

## 9. Stamp Generation

A simplified Hashcash minting procedure is:

```text
Input:
- difficulty bits
- date
- resource
- random value

Initialize counter

↓

Construct stamp

↓

Hash complete stamp

↓

Does hash satisfy difficulty?

├── No  → increment counter and repeat
└── Yes → publish stamp
```

Pseudocode:

```text
counter = 0

while true:
    stamp = encode(
        version,
        bits,
        date,
        resource,
        extension,
        random_value,
        counter
    )

    digest = SHA1(stamp)

    if has_required_zero_prefix(digest, bits):
        return stamp

    counter += 1
```

This is a probabilistic search.

A generator may find a valid stamp earlier or later than the statistical expectation.

`2^bits` is an expected value, not a guarantee.

## 10. Stamp Verification

A verifier does not repeat the entire search.

It evaluates the completed stamp.

A simplified verification procedure is:

```text
1. Parse the stamp.
2. Confirm the supported version.
3. Confirm the claimed difficulty.
4. Confirm the expected resource.
5. Confirm that the date is acceptable.
6. Hash the stamp once.
7. Confirm that the hash satisfies the work requirement.
8. Confirm that the stamp has not already been used.
```

Pseudocode:

```text
function verify(stamp, expected_resource, minimum_bits, current_time):
    fields = parse(stamp)

    if fields.version != 1:
        return false

    if fields.bits < minimum_bits:
        return false

    if fields.resource != expected_resource:
        return false

    if is_expired(fields.date, current_time):
        return false

    digest = SHA1(stamp)

    if not has_required_zero_prefix(digest, fields.bits):
        return false

    if replay_database.contains(stamp):
        return false

    replay_database.insert(stamp)

    return true
```

The exact policy depends on the application.

A syntactically valid Proof of Work is not automatically acceptable if:

- the resource is wrong,
- the stamp is expired,
- the claimed difficulty is insufficient,
- the stamp has already been used.

## 11. Partial Preimage, Not a Full Collision

Hashcash is sometimes described as searching for a "partial hash collision."

That terminology appears in historical descriptions, but it can mislead readers familiar with modern cryptographic terminology.

### 11.1 Full Collision

A cryptographic collision means finding two distinct inputs:

```text
x ≠ y
```

such that:

```text
H(x) = H(y)
```

Hashcash does not require this.

### 11.2 Hashcash Search

Hashcash searches for an input whose hash satisfies a pattern:

```text
H(x) begins with b zero bits
```

This is more accurately understood as a partial preimage-style search or constrained hash-output search.

The generator is not attempting to match another input's complete hash.

It is attempting to find any input whose output lies inside a sufficiently small target set.

---

## 12. Difficulty and Expected Work

Assume a cryptographic hash behaves like a uniformly random function.

For a requirement of `b` leading zero bits:

```text
P(success per trial) = 1 / 2^b
```

The number of trials follows a geometric distribution.

The expected number of trials is:

```text
E[trials] = 2^b
```

### Example: 20 Bits

```text
P(success) = 1 / 2^20
           = 1 / 1,048,576
```

The generator therefore expects to evaluate roughly one million candidates.

The verifier evaluates the final candidate once.

This is the core asymmetry:

```text
Generation cost ≈ 2^b hashes

Verification cost ≈ 1 hash
```

Additional parsing, timestamp, resource, and replay checks are required, but they do not scale with the original search effort.

---

## 13. Why Leading Zeros?

Leading zero bits are not economically or cryptographically valuable by themselves.

They are a convenient representation of a target condition.

Instead of saying:

```text
hash must be less than target T
```

Hashcash can express difficulty as:

```text
hash must begin with b zero bits
```

These ideas are closely related.

A requirement for more leading zero bits reduces the number of acceptable outputs and increases expected work.

Bitcoin later generalized this into a numeric target comparison.

---

## 14. Replay Resistance

A valid stamp is merely data.

Without additional checks, it can be copied.

Hashcash therefore requires application-level replay controls.

### 14.1 Resource Binding

The stamp includes the intended resource.

This prevents reuse across unrelated recipients or services.

### 14.2 Date Binding

The stamp includes a date.

This limits the period during which it may be accepted.

### 14.3 Replay Database

The verifier may store previously accepted stamps.

A duplicate is rejected.

### 14.4 Important Limitation

Proof of Work does not inherently make a token single-use.

Single-use behavior emerges from:

- the stamp format,
- verifier policy,
- resource binding,
- expiry rules,
- replay tracking.

This distinction becomes important when comparing Hashcash with Bitcoin's UTXO model.

---

## 15. Interactive and Non-Interactive Cost Functions

The 2002 Hashcash report distinguishes between interactive and non-interactive settings.

### 15.1 Interactive Model

A server issues a fresh challenge.

The client computes a proof specific to that challenge.

```text
Server

↓

Challenge

↓

Client computes work

↓

Proof

↓

Server verifies
```

Advantages:

- fresh challenge reduces replay risk,
- the server controls when the puzzle is issued.

Limitations:

- requires an interactive connection,
- may add protocol round trips,
- the server must participate before work begins.

### 15.2 Non-Interactive Model

The client creates a stamp without first receiving a server challenge.

Email is a store-and-forward system, making non-interactive proofs useful.

The stamp must therefore include sufficient context to limit reuse:

- date,
- resource,
- random value,
- counter.

Bitcoin mining is also non-interactive in the sense that miners construct candidate block headers and independently search for valid Proof of Work without receiving a personalized challenge from a central server.

The current chain tip and consensus target collectively define the puzzle context.

---

## 16. Security Assumptions

Hashcash relies on several assumptions.

### 16.1 Hash Output Uniformity

Hash outputs are assumed to be sufficiently uniform that no candidate strategy substantially outperforms repeated search.

### 16.2 No Efficient Shortcut

The generator is assumed not to have an algorithm that finds acceptable outputs substantially faster than brute-force search.

### 16.3 Verification Is Cheap

The verifier must be able to validate the result far more cheaply than the generator produced it.

### 16.4 Required Work Is Economically Meaningful

The selected difficulty must be high enough to affect abusive senders.

A difficulty that is trivial on current hardware provides little protection.

### 16.5 Resource Binding Is Enforced

The verifier must confirm that the stamp was minted for the correct resource.

### 16.6 Replay Controls Exist

The application must reject reused or expired stamps.

---

## 17. Engineering Trade-offs

### 17.1 Honest-User Friction

Proof of Work imposes a cost on all users, not only attackers.

An honest sender must also perform the computation.

### 17.2 Hardware Inequality

The same difficulty does not impose the same time or monetary burden on every participant.

A user with specialized or faster hardware can produce proofs more cheaply.

### 17.3 Energy Use

Computational pricing intentionally consumes resources.

This is not an accidental side effect.

The resource cost is the deterrent.

### 17.4 Botnet Risk

An attacker may externalize costs by using compromised machines.

### 17.5 Difficulty Calibration

If difficulty is too low, abuse remains cheap.

If difficulty is too high, legitimate use becomes inconvenient.

### 17.6 Replay-State Cost

Preventing duplicate use may require the verifier to maintain a database of accepted stamps.

---

## 18. Hashcash vs Bitcoin Proof of Work

Bitcoin's Whitepaper states that its distributed timestamp server requires a Proof-of-Work system similar to Adam Back's Hashcash.

"Similar" is the critical word.

Bitcoin inherits the search structure but changes its purpose and surrounding protocol.

| Dimension | Hashcash | Bitcoin |
|---|---|---|
| Primary purpose | Throttle resource abuse | Secure distributed transaction history |
| Original use case | Email and service abuse | Permissionless electronic cash |
| Work object | Textual stamp | Serialized block header |
| Historical hash function | SHA-1 | Double SHA-256 |
| Difficulty expression | Required zero bits | Numeric target encoded in `nBits` |
| Search field | Stamp counter and mutable fields | Nonce plus other mutable header/block-template data |
| Verification | Hash stamp and validate fields | Hash block header and compare with target |
| Replay protection | Application policy/database | Consensus rules and block/transaction structure |
| Chain selection | None | Greatest cumulative chain work |
| Economic reward | Not inherent | Block subsidy and transaction fees |
| Difficulty adjustment | Application-defined | Consensus-defined retargeting |
| Consensus | Not provided | Nakamoto Consensus |

---

## 19. What Bitcoin Inherited

Bitcoin inherited several central ideas.

### 19.1 Probabilistic Hash Search

Both systems repeatedly vary input data and hash it until an acceptable output is found.

### 19.2 Adjustable Difficulty

Both systems can change the size of the acceptable output set.

### 19.3 Cheap Verification

A completed proof is inexpensive to verify.

### 19.4 Computational Scarcity

Valid proofs cannot be produced freely in unlimited quantities.

### 19.5 Public Auditability

A third party can independently verify that work satisfying the rule was performed.

---

## 20. What Bitcoin Added

Bitcoin added the structures necessary to transform a cost function into a consensus mechanism.

### 20.1 Block Commitment

The work commits to a block header representing a specific block.

### 20.2 Previous-Block Commitment

Each block header references the previous block hash.

### 20.3 Transaction Commitment

The Merkle root commits to the block's transactions.

### 20.4 Chain Selection

Nodes compare competing histories using accumulated Proof of Work.

### 20.5 Consensus Validation

Nodes independently validate both:

- Proof of Work,
- all applicable block and transaction rules.

### 20.6 Economic Incentives

Successful block production may earn:

- block subsidy,
- transaction fees.

### 20.7 Automatic Difficulty Adjustment

Bitcoin periodically adjusts its target to maintain a desired average block interval.

Hashcash itself does not provide this complete system.

## 21. Bitcoin's Target Model

The Bitcoin Whitepaper explains Proof of Work using a hash beginning with a required number of zero bits.

Modern Bitcoin validation is more precisely expressed as:

```text
block_header_hash ≤ target
```

A lower target means:

- fewer acceptable hash outputs,
- greater expected work,
- higher difficulty.

The apparent leading zeros in displayed block hashes are a visual consequence of a low numeric hash value.

Bitcoin does not merely count a fixed number of zero characters in a displayed hexadecimal string.

---

## 22. Bitcoin Core Mapping

Bitcoin Core's Proof-of-Work validation is centered on logic in:

```text
src/pow.cpp
```

Conceptually, validation performs the following steps:

```text
nBits

↓

Decode compact target

↓

Reject invalid target:
- negative
- zero
- overflow
- above proof-of-work limit

↓

Convert block hash to integer

↓

Check:

hash ≤ target
```

A simplified representation is:

```cpp
target = DeriveTarget(nBits, consensus_pow_limit);

if (!target) {
    return false;
}

if (block_hash > target) {
    return false;
}

return true;
```

### Important Interpretation

Bitcoin Core does not verify that a miner spent a particular amount of electricity.

It verifies that the submitted block header hash satisfies the consensus target.

The expended work is inferred statistically from the improbability of obtaining such a hash.

---

## 23. Why Verification Does Not Prove Exact Cost

A valid Proof of Work proves that an acceptable result was found.

It does not reveal:

- the exact number of attempts,
- the hardware used,
- the electricity consumed,
- whether the miner was lucky,
- the miner's precise monetary cost.

A miner might find a valid result on the first attempt.

Another miner might require far more than the expected number of attempts.

Proof of Work therefore proves possession of a statistically difficult result, not an auditable invoice for a fixed amount of energy.

---

## 24. Hashcash Is Not Consensus

Hashcash solves a cost-imposition problem.

It does not independently answer:

- Which transactions are valid?
- Which transaction was seen first?
- Which chain should nodes follow?
- How are conflicting histories resolved?
- Who receives newly issued currency?
- How does difficulty adapt?
- What prevents invalid blocks from being accepted?

Bitcoin answers these through a larger protocol.

A useful relationship is:

```text
Hashcash-style puzzle

+

Block structure

+

Peer-to-peer network

+

Independent validation

+

Cumulative-work chain selection

+

Economic incentives

+

Difficulty adjustment

=

Bitcoin Proof-of-Work consensus system
```

## 25. On-Chain Implications

Hashcash itself does not produce Bitcoin on-chain data.

However, the design principle it contributed appears in several observable Bitcoin variables.

### 25.1 Block Hash

Each accepted block has a header hash satisfying the target.

### 25.2 `nBits`

The block header records the compact representation of the target.

### 25.3 Difficulty

Difficulty is derived from the relationship between the current target and a reference target.

### 25.4 Block Interval

Observed block times reflect the probabilistic interaction between:

- network hash rate,
- current target.

### 25.5 Chainwork

Bitcoin nodes track accumulated expected work when comparing valid chains.

### 25.6 Mining Economics

Hash-based work creates observable economic relationships involving:

- miner revenue,
- electricity cost,
- hardware efficiency,
- hash rate,
- fee income,
- block subsidy.

These subjects are analyzed in later Research Units.

## 26. Institutional Thinking

### Observation

Bitcoin cites Hashcash as the model for its Proof-of-Work mechanism.

### Weak Interpretation

> Bitcoin simply uses Hashcash.

### Institutional Interpretation

The research question should be decomposed.

1. Which computational primitive was inherited?
2. Which hash function changed?
3. What data is committed by the proof?
4. How is difficulty expressed?
5. How is the proof incorporated into chain selection?
6. Which economic incentives surround the proof?
7. Which consensus rules remain independent of Proof of Work?

### Conclusion

Hashcash explains Bitcoin's cost-asymmetric search primitive.

It does not by itself explain Bitcoin consensus.

## 27. Analyst Notes

When evaluating a Proof-of-Work protocol, check:

### Puzzle

- What data is hashed?
- Which fields can be varied?
- Which hash function is used?
- What defines a valid result?

### Difficulty

- How is the target represented?
- How often is it adjusted?
- Who determines it?
- Are emergency or minimum-difficulty rules present?

### Verification

- Can all nodes verify cheaply?
- Is verification deterministic?
- Which invalid-target conditions are checked?

### Consensus

- How are competing chains compared?
- Is block count used, or cumulative work?
- Can high-work invalid blocks be accepted?

### Economics

- Who performs the work?
- Who pays the cost?
- Who receives the reward?
- Can costs be externalized?

### Security

- Can proofs be replayed?
- Are proofs bound to specific data?
- Can specialized hardware dominate?
- What happens if one entity controls most work production?

## 28. Common Misinterpretations

### Misinterpretation 1

> Hashcash and Bitcoin mining are the same protocol.

Incorrect.

They share a hash-search principle but differ in data structures, objectives, consensus integration, incentives, and difficulty governance.

---

### Misinterpretation 2

> Hashcash finds hash collisions.

Imprecise.

It searches for an input whose hash satisfies a restricted output condition. It does not require two distinct inputs with identical full hashes.

---

### Misinterpretation 3

> A valid Proof of Work proves an exact amount of energy was spent.

Incorrect.

It proves that a statistically difficult result was found under the assumed hash-function model.

---

### Misinterpretation 4

> More leading zeros are the fundamental Bitcoin consensus rule.

Incomplete.

Bitcoin Core interprets `nBits` as a numeric target and checks whether the block hash is less than or equal to that target.

---

### Misinterpretation 5

> Proof of Work alone prevents Double Spending.

Incorrect.

Double-spend resistance depends on the interaction of Proof of Work with transaction validation, block linkage, chain selection, network propagation, and confirmation depth.

---

### Misinterpretation 6

> Proof of Work identifies honest participants.

Incorrect.

Proof of Work is not an identity system.

It weights block-production influence by demonstrated computational work.

---

## 29. Counter Evidence and Limitations

Hashcash's anti-abuse objective has important limitations.

A computational fee does not affect every user equally.

Attackers may possess:

- faster hardware,
- cheaper electricity,
- botnets,
- specialized processors.

Meanwhile, legitimate users may operate:

- low-power devices,
- mobile hardware,
- accessibility-limited systems.

Therefore, computational pricing does not automatically produce fair access.

It creates one type of scarcity while introducing hardware and resource inequalities.

This limitation becomes even more important in permissionless cryptocurrency mining, where specialized hardware and energy markets shape participation.
