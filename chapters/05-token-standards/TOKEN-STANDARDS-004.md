## 1. Learning Objectives

After completing this Research Unit, readers should be able to:

- Define a stablecoin as a tokenized claim or mechanism targeting stable purchasing or reference value.
- Distinguish fiat-backed, crypto-backed, and algorithmic stabilization approaches.
- Explain why ERC-20 transferability is not the same as credible redemption.
- Separate peg maintenance from reserve transparency.
- Identify the main failure surfaces in stablecoin design.

## 3. Executive Summary

Ethereum.org describes stablecoins as Ethereum tokens designed to stay at a fixed value even when ETH's price changes.[^ref-eth-stablecoins]

That user-facing description is useful but incomplete for research. A stablecoin is not defined only by a stable price target. It is defined by a stabilization system that tries to keep the token near a reference asset or unit, typically through:

- redeemability,
- collateralization,
- issuance controls,
- arbitrage incentives,
- and sometimes governance intervention.

Circle describes USDC as a digital dollar backed 100% by highly liquid cash and cash-equivalent assets and redeemable 1:1 for USD.[^ref-circle-usdc]

Maker documentation describes Dai as the stablecoin generated by the Maker Protocol against approved crypto collateral assets.[^ref-maker-overview][^ref-maker-dai]

These examples show the main analytical split:

- some stablecoins stabilize through issuer-managed reserves and redemption,
- others stabilize through smart-contract collateral systems and liquidation logic.

In both cases, the token may use an ERC-20 interface, but the ERC-20 layer is only the transport layer. The real research question is whether the stabilization architecture remains solvent, liquid, and trusted under stress.

## 4. System Structure

### 4.1 Stablecoin as a Layered Object

A stablecoin usually combines at least four layers:

1. token interface layer,
2. issuance and redemption layer,
3. collateral or reserve layer,
4. governance and policy layer.

ERC-20 standardization only covers the first layer.

### 4.2 Major Design Families

Ethereum.org groups stablecoins by mechanism, including fiat-backed, crypto-backed, precious-metal-backed, and algorithmic designs.[^ref-eth-stablecoins]

For research purposes, the most important distinction is between:

- externally collateralized redeemable claims,
- internally collateralized overcollateralized systems,
- and reflexive algorithmic systems.

### 4.3 Example: Fiat-Backed

Circle states that USDC is designed to represent US dollars on the internet and is redeemable 1:1 for USD.[^ref-circle-usdc]

This model depends on:

- issuer operations,
- banking and reserve custody,
- attestation processes,
- and redemption access.

### 4.4 Example: Crypto-Backed

Maker documentation explains that the Maker Protocol allows users to generate Dai against approved crypto collateral assets and that collateral types and risk parameters are governed by MKR holders.[^ref-maker-overview]

This model depends on:

- collateral valuation,
- liquidation infrastructure,
- interest-rate policy,
- and protocol governance.

## 5. Peg Mechanisms

### 5.1 Redemption Anchor

For redeemable fiat-backed stablecoins, the peg is reinforced by the expectation that users can convert the token into fiat at or near par, subject to issuer rules.

If token price falls below par, arbitrage can buy discounted tokens and redeem them. If price rises above par, new issuance and sale may restore parity.

### 5.2 Overcollateralized Debt Model

Maker's documentation describes Dai as part of a system with multiple collateral types, stability fees, and stabilizing mechanisms.[^ref-maker-overview][^ref-maker-rates][^ref-maker-stabilizer]

In this model, the peg is not primarily guaranteed by issuer redemption into bank deposits. It is supported by:

- collateral backing,
- liquidation,
- parameter governance,
- and system recapitalization processes.

### 5.3 Algorithmic or Reflexive Mechanisms

Ethereum.org notes that some stablecoins rely on smart-contract algorithms rather than reserve-backed structures.[^ref-eth-stablecoins]

These designs are typically more reflexive because peg confidence may depend on future demand, token incentives, or governance credibility rather than direct reserve redemption.

## 6. Mathematical or Economic Model

### 6.1 Simple Fiat-Backed Solvency Condition

Let:

- `R` = redeemable reserve assets
- `L` = stablecoin liabilities outstanding

A minimal solvency condition is:

`R >= L`

If immediate redeemability is promised at par, liquidity quality matters as much as nominal reserve size.

### 6.2 Overcollateralized System Condition

For collateral asset set `C`, collateral values `V_i`, debt `D`, and required collateralization ratio `m > 1`:

`sum(V_i) >= m * D`

must hold at the position level or system level depending on design.

Liquidation systems exist because collateral value can move faster than governance or user response.

### 6.3 Peg vs Solvency

A token can temporarily trade near peg while hidden insolvency builds, and it can temporarily trade off peg even if long-run solvency remains intact. Price stability and balance-sheet strength are related but not identical observables.

## 7. Security Considerations

### 7.1 Reserve Opacity

For fiat-backed models, token transfers are fully visible onchain while reserve assets usually are not. This creates a mismatch between onchain transparency and offchain balance-sheet dependency.

### 7.2 Oracle and Liquidation Risk

For crypto-backed systems, incorrect prices, delayed liquidations, or poor collateral governance can weaken the peg or produce insolvency.

### 7.3 Redemption Access Risk

A stablecoin can remain technically transferable onchain while redemption is restricted, delayed, or unavailable to many users. That distinction matters in stress events.

### 7.4 Governance Risk

Maker documentation makes clear that collateral types and risk parameters are governance-managed.[^ref-maker-overview]

That means peg stability is partly a governance quality question, not just a code question.

---

## 8. Implementation Notes

Circle's documentation positions USDC as a multi-chain digital dollar with reserve attestations and 1:1 redeemability.[^ref-circle-usdc]

Maker's documentation positions Dai as the user-facing ERC-20 contract sitting above a broader protocol architecture with join adapters, collateral modules, rates, and stabilization systems.[^ref-maker-dai][^ref-maker-rates][^ref-maker-stabilizer]

The practical lesson is that "stablecoin contract" can be a misleading phrase. In serious systems, the token contract is often the simplest visible component of a much larger operational or protocol machine.

## 9. On-Chain Implications

### 9.1 Stablecoins as Settlement Assets

Stablecoins often function as:

- exchange quote assets,
- DeFi collateral,
- payment balances,
- and treasury units.

### 9.2 Transfer Data Is Incomplete

Onchain flows show circulation patterns, concentration, and protocol usage, but they do not directly prove reserve sufficiency or redemption fairness.

### 9.3 Stress Monitoring

Analysts should monitor:

- peg deviations,
- issuance and redemption changes,
- collateral composition changes,
- governance parameter updates,
- and concentration in key counterparties or protocols.

---

## 10. Institutional Thinking

- Treat stablecoins as monetary systems, not only as ERC-20 contracts.
- Separate interface compliance from reserve quality.
- Separate nominal peg from actual exit liquidity.
- In fiat-backed models, ask who holds reserves, who can redeem, how quickly, and under what legal conditions.
- In crypto-backed models, ask what collateral is accepted, how liquidation works, how losses are socialized, and how governance responds under stress.
